## When is Oktoberfest 2026?

The **191st Oktoberfest** opens on **Saturday 19 September 2026** and runs until **Sunday 4 October 2026**. The festival begins at noon on the first day, when the Mayor of Munich taps the first keg in the Schottenhaml tent with the traditional cry of *"O'zapft is!"* ("It's tapped!") and the beer can officially flow. The morning after, on **Sunday 20 September**, the Costume and Riflemen's Parade — around 9,000 people in authentic Bavarian *Tracht* — winds through the city to the Theresienwiese. The final weekend, closing on **3–4 October** around German Unity Day, is the busiest of the whole run, so book your transport early if those are your dates.

The Wiesn opens daily from the morning, with the tents serving through the afternoon and evening. Weekends and the two closing days draw the biggest crowds; a weekday visit is calmer if you have the flexibility to choose.

## How far is Munich from Prague?

Munich sits about **385 kilometres southwest of Prague**, across the Czech-German border into Bavaria. In a private car the realistic door-to-door time is **around four hours** in clean conditions. The drive is almost entirely motorway: the D5 out of Prague past Plzeň, across the border at Rozvadov/Waidhaus, then the German A6 and A93 south past Regensburg, joining the A9 for the final approach into the city.

By comparison, the train from Prague to Munich takes roughly five and a half to six hours with the scenery of a slow regional line, and it drops a group at the Hauptbahnhof with their bags to sort out the last mile themselves. The express coach is cheaper but slower again, and tied to a fixed timetable that rarely matches when your group actually wants to leave. For getting a party of friends to the Wiesn in one piece, on their own schedule, the private car is in a different category.

## The route, from Prague to the Theresienwiese

The drive is straightforward and fast. Your chauffeur leaves central Prague on the **D5 motorway**, running southwest past **Plzeň**, then crosses the Czech-German border at **Rozvadov/Waidhaus**. On the German side the route picks up the **A6**, turns south on the **A93** past **Regensburg**, and joins the **A9** for the run into central Munich. Total distance is about **385 kilometres**, roughly four hours in normal traffic.

Two things can add time, and a good driver plans around both. The **A99 ring** around Munich is heavy at rush hour, so an arrival between 07:30–09:30 or 16:30–18:30 can add twenty to thirty minutes. And during Oktoberfest itself the streets around the **Theresienwiese** are closed or restricted, so drop-off is arranged at the nearest permitted point with a short, clearly directed walk to the gates. Your chauffeur watches the traffic and the festival road closures before every departure and reroutes without being asked.

## Getting home safely — same day or overnight

The return is the half of the trip that a private car handles best. Two patterns cover almost every group:

- **Same-day return.** Head down in the morning, spend the afternoon and early evening at the Wiesn, and your chauffeur brings the group back to Prague the same night — everyone asleep in the cabin instead of awake at the wheel. A same-day return is arranged as one booking, and the return leg comes at a reduced rate when booked together.
- **Stay over.** Many groups make a weekend of it. The chauffeur drops you at your Munich hotel, and you arrange a separate return whenever suits — the next morning, or after a second day in the city. Munich has plenty beyond the tents: Marienplatz and the Glockenspiel, the English Garden, the museum quarter in Maxvorstadt, and the BMW Welt.

Either way, the principle is the same: nobody in the group has to think about driving, parking, or catching the last train. That is the whole idea.
